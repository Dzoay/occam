# `proving-ground.p9`: how should tessellation parallelise?

In short: sample every shared edge once, then mesh faces independently against those fixed samples, one task per face, with
lanes inside each task. On the measured models that was watertight on every output where every face meshed, and the same bits
at every thread count and lane width. It reached 4.7 to 5.2 times at six threads, and neither the edge phase nor any single
face limited that. WebGPU compute does not belong in `occam-mesh`: its results are 32-bit and differ between GPUs, and a
tessellator's batches are too small to pay for the round trip. The method needs hard limits on what a surface may ask of a
shared edge: without them, one collapsed NURBS row took 12 GB of a 15 GB laptop in seconds.

Code: `spikes/p9/` on branch `spike-p9`. Raw output is in `spikes/p9/results/`, each file headed by the command that made it.
`spikes/p9/run/measure.sh` holds those commands and `spikes/p9/run/tables.py` regenerates the tables below.

## Question

How should tessellation parallelise? The candidates are shared edges first and then faces independently, watertight by
construction, and CPU lanes against WebGPU compute. It feeds `occam-mesh` and the renderer. The full statement is in
`spikes/p9/QUESTION.md`.

## What was measured

**Machine.** Every timing not marked "laptop" ran on the measuring machine: an Intel Core i7-8700K, six physical cores (CPUs 0
to 5 used, hyperthreads never), governor `powersave`. Single-core turbo is above all-core turbo, so speed-ups read a little
low; this is stated and not corrected for. `rustc 1.99.0`, target `x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu`, `RUSTFLAGS` unset except where
a build says otherwise, `lto = "fat"`, `codegen-units = 1`, no `target-cpu`. `rayon 1.12.0`. Firefox 159 headless, with a
throwaway profile and pages served on localhost only. wasmtime ran the wasm lane benchmarks. The laptop (Intel Core i7-1165G7,
Iris Xe, Chromium 156 and Firefox 159) is not quiet and is used only to compare GPU adapters.

**The tessellator.** Throwaway and written for this question. Phase 0 reads each face's size and curvature from an 8 by 8 grid
of surface samples. Phase 1 samples every edge once, adaptively, by chord height and turning angle (mid-point subdivision, as
in Lane and Riesenfeld, 1980), to half the tightest deflection of the faces that use it. Phase 2 meshes every face against
those fixed samples: a constrained Delaunay triangulation over a 2^27 integer grid in the surface's own metric (Lawson, 1977;
Sloan, 1993; a walk from the last triangle located, walking strategies as in Devillers, Pion and Teillaud, 2002; the integer grid makes `orient2d` and `incircle` exact, compare
Shewchuk, 1997), refined in waves that evaluate the surface in one batch per wave (Piegl and Tiller, *The NURBS Book*, 2nd
ed., 1997, A2.1 to A2.3). Phase 3 merges into flat typed arrays by index (L33): welded `f64` positions, `u32` indices, per-face
triangle ranges and per-corner `f32` normals. Boundary vertex numbers are known before any face runs, so nothing is
coordinated between faces.

**Models.** A deterministic mix of closed parts: plates with holes, solids of revolution (sphere, torus, cylinder, cone, bolts
with fillets and poles), bicubic NURBS panels with a trimmed hole, and bicubic and rational biquadratic quilts on a torus. Sizes
are 1,002, 10,001 and 100,023 faces, and a model of 1,000 faces plus one plate whose top and bottom each have 4,096 holes.

**Checks on every output.** Every undirected edge of the welded mesh must be used exactly twice in opposite directions, with
no degenerate triangle, and V - E + F must equal the sum of the parts' Euler characteristics. A negative control
(`tests/negative_control.rs`, debug build, so the triangulation's assertions are live) removes a triangle, flips one, doubles
one, collapses one, and splits a vertex in two: the check reports each.

**Quality.** Chordal error is the distance from the true surface to the triangle, at 28 points per triangle (Ericson,
*Real-Time Collision Detection*, 2005, 5.1.5). Angular deflection compares the triangle's normal with the surface normal at the
same points. Sampling gives a lower bound on the true maximum, not a proof.

**Protocol.** Seven repeats per configuration, minimum and median, one-thread runs of at least 100 ms, output hashed with
`occam_math::Digest` after the clock stops, pinned with `taskset -c 0-<n-1>`, through `spikes/tools/remote.sh`. The work runs on
a pool thread at every thread count, including one. Large native runs carried `ulimit -v`; browser runs had a timeout only.

**Binaries.** Timings come from commit `23e0ae9`. The big-plate results come from `60082dd`, which fixes a bug described below.
Rerunning the 1,002-face model on the fixed binary gave the same digest, so the fix does not change the other results. The
final quality tables come from `913993d`, which adds a checker field and a runtime parameter whose default is unchanged.

## Results

### Watertight by construction, and how far that holds

Of 48 adversarial cases, 45 were watertight with the right Euler characteristic and three failed, all reported. Each row is
one or more cases from `results/adv.txt` (commit `23e0ae9`), `adv2.txt` (`f19990b`), `adv3.txt` (`60082dd`) and `adv4.txt` (`913993d` plus the
case it adds, run on the laptop under the memory cap, with no timing in it).

| Family | Cases | Range | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seams and degenerate points: two-pole sphere, torus with two seams, cone apex, cylinder, bolt, NURBS patch with a collapsed edge | 6 | | All watertight |
| Sliver walls: plate thickness | 6 | 1e-1 to 1e-11 | All watertight |
| Strip width | 5 | 1e-2 to 1e-10 | All watertight |
| Crescent, two arcs nearly coincident, walls asking 50:1 different resolutions | 6 | width 1e-1 to 1e-6 | All watertight |
| Needle triangle, walls asking 100:1 | 4 | tip 1e-1 to 1e-7 rad | All watertight |
| Hole next to an arc that bulges towards it | 5 | gap 1e-1 to 1e-7 | 4 watertight; **gap 1e-7 failed** |
| Hole under the middle of a chord, so the coarse chord cuts it | 7 | gap 1e-1 to 1e-8 | All watertight after one repair round |
| One edge of length 1e-k between long ones | 4 | 1e-2 to 1e-10 | 2 watertight; **1e-8 and 1e-10 failed** |
| Neighbouring faces asking very different resolutions | 4 | 100:1, 10,000:1 | All watertight, up to 20.8 million triangles |
| One plate with 1,024 holes | 1 | | Watertight |

The three failures are one thing: two boundary points landed on one cell of the integer grid, at or below about 2^-27 of the face's size. The
face is reported as failed, its neighbours are unaffected, and the check sees the hole (22, 10 and 10 open edges). "By
construction" holds only for faces that mesh: the guarantee is that independent faces cannot disagree along an edge, and a
face that cannot be triangulated still leaves a hole.

Writing the catalogue also found defects in the method itself:

- **Unbounded memory, and the reason for this finding's first warning.** A bicubic patch with a collapsed row reached 11.9 GB on
  the laptop within seconds. By reasoning from the code, confirmed by the fix but not separately instrumented: on the collapsed
  row the derivative is rounding noise, so the "normal" is a unit vector in a random direction; the size estimate read that as
  a curvature near 1e15; the wanted edge length became about 3e-8; and the edge sampler was bounded only by 40 waves, which
  allow 2^40 intervals, so one edge asked for about 2^28 samples. The fix has three parts: ignore grid points that coincide,
  never ask for a size below the deflection, and cap each edge at 2^14 samples. **This is a property of the method.** A shared
  edge takes the tightest size any of its faces asks for, so one face's bad estimate is paid for by every neighbour, and an
  unbounded sampler turns it into an allocation failure for the whole model.
- **Repair amplifies.** A bug of mine (a 4,096-step limit walking around a vertex, which a plate corner exceeds) made two faces
  fail. Repair then re-sampled every edge of those faces at a quarter of the tolerance per round, and the model went from 2.2
  million triangles and 1 s to 17.8 million triangles and 144 s. Repair has to name the edges that collided, and only for
  failures that finer samples can cure. Finer samples make a grid collision worse.
- **Chordal error must be measured to the triangle.** Comparing a surface point with the parametrically corresponding point on
  the triangle counts uneven parametrisation as error; refinement then chased it into boundary slivers and never converged.
- **Conical points** cannot satisfy an angular test at any size, so refinement needs a floor.

### Quality

The 1,002-face model, and the 10,001-face model in the last row (`results/quality2.txt`, `quality3.txt`). The angular limit is 0.35 rad.

| Deflection | Triangles | Max deviation / deflection | Triangles over deflection | Max angle (rad) | Triangles over angular | Of which under 1 degree | Area over angular | Mean smallest angle | Under 20 degrees |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 96,576 | 0.815 | 0 | 3.03 | 6,416 (6.6%) | 1,895 | 1.02% | 28.5 | 31% |
| 0.02 | 454,558 | 0.806 | 0 | 0.46 | 142 (0.03%) | 0 | 0.0018% | 29.0 | 31% |
| 0.005 | 2,055,908 | 0.770 | 0 | 3.14 | 2,178 (0.1%) | 2,178 | under 0.0001% | 26.0 | 40% |
| 0.2 (10,001 faces) | 1,179,318 | 0.832 | 0 | 3.14 | 217,149 (18%) | 113,363 | 3.66% | 19.0 | 55% |

The stated chordal deflection was met at every level and on every kind of surface, and it held on all checked adversarial
cases (at most 0.81; the two 10,000:1 cases were checked for watertightness only). The angular target is not reliably met,
and at coarse deflection it is well off: at 0.2, triangles as bad as inverted cover 3.66% of the area (13.9% on cylinders and
cones). Most of what is over the limit at fine deflection is slivers whose normal means little: at 0.005, all of it, on a
negligible area. A smaller refinement floor was tried as the cause and is not: a tenth of the floor at deflection 0.1 gives 1.7
times the triangles and exactly the same 1.02%. The refinement tests the angle at four points per triangle and the check at 28,
including the corners, which is the likely reason, but that was not tested.

Planes are not refined, so their triangles are poor by design: mean smallest angle 2.1 to 11.4 degrees. Nothing here needs them
better, but a renderer should not assume good shape. Also, 84 of 100,023 faces in the largest model, 9 of 10,001 at deflection
0.2, and 13 faces in the 10,000:1 cases stopped at the refinement limit, without losing watertightness.

### Parallel scaling

Median ms of the whole pipeline, with speed-up against one thread on medians. Minimum-based speed-ups are within 4% of these,
except the last row at six threads.

| Model | Triangles | 1 | 2 | 4 | 6 | Speed-up at 2 | at 4 | at 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,002 faces, deflection 0.02 | 454,558 | 1150 | 633 | 329 | 223 | 1.82 | 3.49 | **5.17** |
| 10,001 faces, 0.05 | 1,850,878 | 4289 | 2362 | 1198 | 819 | 1.82 | 3.58 | **5.24** |
| 100,023 faces, 0.2 | 11,451,560 | 26197 | 14105 | 7829 | 5586 | 1.86 | 3.35 | **4.69** |
| 5,104 faces, two with 4,096 holes | 2,178,858 | 4979 | 2639 | 1515 | 1018 | 1.89 | 3.29 | **4.89** |

Median was within 5% of minimum everywhere except the last row at six threads (9%): six of the seven runs took 1011 to 1050 ms,
and one took 934 ms. I did not find what made that run faster, so the median is the figure to use.

**Bit-identical.** Each model produced one digest across all 28 runs at 1, 2, 4 and 6 threads, every one watertight. The 1,002-face
model was also hashed at threads 3 and 5 and at lane widths 1, 2, 4 and 8: one digest (`9efa6a7df2e6a070`).

**Where the time goes**, one thread, 1,002 faces: surface evaluation 45%, deciding and inserting points 41.5%, the boundary
triangulation 3.6%, the rest (set-up, output, final normals) about 10%. The edge phase and face analysis together are 1.1% of
the run at 1,002 faces and 2.6% at 100,023. So edges first is free: at six threads the edge phase speeds up about 4 to 5 times, but
it is too small to matter, and the faces speed up 4.8 to 5.3 times.

**How the work divides.** One task per face, dynamically scheduled: per-thread busy time was within 2.3% at 10,001 faces and
3.5% at 1,002. The largest face is 0.7% of the work at 10,001 faces, 6.5% at 1,002, and 2.8% for the 4,096-hole plate. Neither
needs a face split across threads at these sizes.

Natively, batching by kind and degree (L29) is worth 1.8 to 3.3 times on analytic surfaces against one point at a time, and 1.2 to
1.4 times on NURBS. On NURBS, width 2 is the most it is worth having, and wider is often slower. Control points are grouped by knot
span, so 256 random points over a 13 by 13 span patch leave one or two points per group, which is a harsh case: real waves are
denser. Lanes wider than the hardware's 128 bits gained 14% on cylinders (width 2 to 8) and nothing elsewhere, in line with p10.

Wasm simd128 was not faster than wasm scalar here in most rows: at width 2, cylinder 25.5 against 24.8, sphere 30.4 against 29.7,
torus 30.2 against 30.3, degree 3 115.8 against 119.5. It was not slower either, so L30 did not recur. All 286 configurations
of surface, normals, batch and width gave the same digest on native, wasm simd128 and wasm scalar, including those using `sin` and
`cos`, because these are computed by a Taylor polynomial over `Real`.

### WebGPU compute against CPU lanes

It runs. Firefox 159 reached a real adapter headless on both the measuring machine and the laptop; Firefox hides the adapter's
name, and the limits differ between the two (`results/probe-*`). Headless Chromium reached only SwiftShader, and one attempt to
give it the real GPU returned no adapter.

Same bicubic B-spline surface (32 by 32 control points), positions and normals on an n by n grid. The shader is a line-for-line
translation of the CPU formula in `f32`. Median ms, measuring machine. "Native" is `Lanes<2>`, one row per task. "GPU busy" comes
from timestamp queries. "Poked" keeps submitting empty work while a round trip is in flight.

| Points | Wasm simd128, 1 thread, in the page | Native 1 thread | Native 6 threads | GPU busy | GPU total, as written | GPU total, poked |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,024 | 0.24 | 0.14 | 0.03 | 0.016 | 200 | 4.5 |
| 10,000 | 1.8 | 0.92 | 0.22 | 0.018 | 200 | 4.6 |
| 99,856 | 13 | 7.2 | 1.5 | 0.105 | 200 | 9.2 |
| 1,000,000 | 134 | 64 | 10.9 | 0.97 | 200 | 22.5 |
| 4,000,000 | 520 | 232 | 43 | 3.9 | 200 | 42.5 |

Four things are visible. The shader itself is fast, about 1 ns per point against 11 for six cores of native `f64`. Getting data
out costs far more: at 4 million points the read-back is 38 of the 42.5 ms. Firefox as written has a floor of about 200 ms per
round trip from what looks like a polling interval, gone when work keeps being submitted. And one wasm thread beats the GPU
up to at least 10,000 points per round trip (1.8 against 4.6 ms), the GPU leads it from about 100,000 (13 against 9.2 ms), native
code on one thread still leads at 100,000, and six native threads match the GPU at 4 million.

SwiftShader is a software renderer and is no faster than wasm: 1,050 ms for a million points on the laptop, against 233 for one
wasm thread. Pipeline compilation was 156 ms in the first browser run and 1.1 ms in the second, in a new profile; the reason was not investigated. Uploading 16 KB of control points measured 77 to 100
ms on Firefox, which is the same polling, and 0.7 ms on SwiftShader.

**Bits (L23).** WGSL has no `f64`, so this is `f32` against `f64`. Of the values at the two grid sizes compared, 19.2 to 21.2% were
identical to the CPU's `f64` rounded to `f32`; the largest absolute difference was 2.4e-6 to 3.5e-6 on a model of about unit size.
Three adapters gave three different digests of the same computation. The same adapter gave the same digest twice. In the probe,
`sin(1)` itself differed in the fourth decimal between adapters (0.84147 against 0.84149). So a GPU result is repeatable on one
machine and differs between machines, and it is not the kernel's result.

**Can it help the tessellator?** Batches average 1,300 to 2,800 points, and 45 to 57% of batches are under 256 points. A batch costs
microseconds on the CPU and at least 4.4 ms on the GPU. Evaluation is 45% of the time, so even free evaluation gains at most 1.8
times, before round trips, and using it would need waves held open across faces, which is exactly the coordination the design
avoids.

## Answer

**`occam-mesh` should sample shared edges first, then mesh faces independently and merge by index.** The edge and analysis phases
take 1 to 3% of the time, it needs no coordination between faces, and the measured results are identical at every thread count and lane width. Speed-up
at six threads was 4.7 to 5.2. One task per face is the right grain at these sizes.

It should run the watertightness check on every output, in production, because "by construction" covers only faces that mesh. It
needs, from the first version:

- A floor on the size any surface may ask of a shared edge, a hard limit on samples per edge and triangles per face, and no
  size estimate from a collapsed row. This is what the 12 GB was.
- Repair that names the colliding edges, and that is not attempted for failures finer samples cannot cure.
- Exact predicates over the real coordinates instead of an integer grid, or a tolerance that merges features below about 1e-8 of
  a face's size before meshing. The grid is the cause of all three failures.
- Chordal error measured to the triangle.

**CPU lanes:** batch by kind and degree, and use width 2 unless a later measurement says otherwise. Width 4 and 8 did not pay on
this build, and L28's composition of threads and lanes works without changing a bit.

**A GPU path is not worth having for `occam-mesh`,** and cannot be part of anything that must be reproducible. It may be worth having
for one thing, which this spike did not measure: display-only surface evaluation at very large point counts, written straight into
a vertex buffer so that the read-back, which is most of the cost, never happens.

**What the renderer can rely on,** if no face is reported as failed: flat typed arrays with welded `f64` positions and `u32` indices;
a closed mesh, every edge used exactly twice in opposite directions, checked and not assumed; per-face triangle ranges; per-corner
unit normals, also at poles; and the stated chordal deflection, as sampled. It cannot rely on triangle shape, nor on the angular
limit at coarse deflection. A renderer that wants smooth shading should use the normals, which come from the surface.

## What this does not show

- The models are synthetic, and parameter curves here are exact lines and circles, so a 3D curve and its parameter curves cannot
  disagree. Real trims can, and that is the classic source of trouble this spike could not see.
- This is a throwaway mesher, so its absolute speed and triangle quality are not the kernel's. The refinement is simple.
- The tessellator itself was not run in the browser or on wasm threads. Only surface evaluation was. `results/` has no
  measurement of a full tessellation on wasm.
- GPU evaluation was one surface kind on a regular grid, not scattered batches, and no large upload was measured. It ran on two
  real GPUs whose names Firefox hides. WebGPU in a page with a render loop was not measured, and that is where the polling floor may
  look different.
- Seven cases produced a real crossing (`boundary-segments-cross`), and one round of repair fixed each, because the refined samples
  happen to land at the closest point. Repair's limit was not found. It was exercised only on these seven small models.
- Chordal error is sampled at 28 points per triangle. It is a lower bound on the true maximum.
- The largest model is at a coarse deflection, 0.2, where the angular result is poor. Its timings say how the pipeline scales, not
  how good that mesh is. The 12 GB chain of reasoning was checked by the fix and not by instrumenting the failing run.
- The cited works were not re-read for this spike. Their authors, years and venues were checked against Crossref and library
  records on 2026-11-09.

## What it decides

For `occam-mesh`: the structure above, the limits, and the check on every output. For the renderer: the contract above. For L23: GPU
results stay out of anything the kernel hashes or reuses. For L28, L29 and L33: all three hold here.

## Open questions for the user

1. Is a GPU display path in scope for the first release? I recommend no: keep the GPU for drawing, and revisit this only if a
   renderer needs dense previews that the CPU cannot feed.
