# P1: where does the time go?

Plan step `proving-ground.p1` ([the milestone](../plan/proving-ground.md)). The code is throwaway, on branch `spike-p1`, in
`spikes/p1/`: a copy of S1 (`spikes/s1/`, not edited) with finer timers and size ladders. Raw rows and the commands are in
`spikes/p1/results/`; `spikes/p1/analyse.py` and `spikes/p1/finding-tables.py` produce the tables below. Nothing here is copied into a crate (L51). Every limit S1
states about itself holds here too: see its finding, `2026-11-09-s1.md`.

## Question

Over the families S1 supports (crossed cylinders, sphere and box, NURBS and plane), where does a boolean spend its time, how
do the shares move as the model grows, what is parallel by nature and what must run in sequence, what is the Amdahl limit at 6
cores and beyond, and which phase sets the ceiling on parallel speed-up?

## What was measured

**The copy.** The boolean of S1, unchanged in what it computes, with timers added: every phase is split into *slices*, and each
slice is either many independent timed tasks or one sequential block. Whatever a phase spends that no task or block accounts for
(its own loops, maps and allocation, plus the timer calls) is charged to a sequential `book` slice of that phase, by subtraction
from the phase's wall time. Setup, which S1 did not time (building every face's trim polygon and the edge-to-face lists), is
timed here. It matters: on the analytic corpus it is 13 to 19% of the boolean, and up to 25% in the plate ladder.

**Runs.** Intel Core i7-8700K, 6 physical cores, governor `powersave`, rustc 1.99.0, `RUSTFLAGS` empty, target
`x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu`, one thread pinned with `taskset -c 0`, through `spikes/tools/remote.sh`, 7 repeats alternating timers
off and on. Off gives the phase shares and whole-boolean times; on gives the tasks and the limits. Result digests were identical
in all repeats of all workloads (`results/*.digests.txt`). The fine timers cost at most 3% against timers off in the run the tables come from (one 16 ms workload, `plate-016-cut`,
showed 5.5% in the other run). Three runs were made: `corpus` and `regions` with one binary (the full list of workloads) and `regions2` with the final one
(the per-region columns; the corpus families and the end points of each ladder). The tables for the corpus, the limits and the
ceiling are from `regions2`; the full ladder table is from `regions`. The commands, and why it took three runs, are in
`results/commands.txt`.

**Workloads.** Corpus families, one per line below: near-tangent crossed cylinders (216 cases, 108 booleans), Steinmetz (88 cases,
52 booleans), near-tangent sphere and box (90 cases, 54 booleans), plates with holes (16 cases, 37 booleans), and S1's NURBS cases
on the debug seed 11 (12 blocks, 3 operations each, 33 of 36 completed; the 3 that returned a report are not counted). One corpus run is below the 100 ms the
protocol asks for (near-sphere, 33 to 36 ms per repeat), and so are the smaller ladder rungs: read those as indicative.

Size ladders, each run as common, cut and fuse (one boolean per size, plate: cut only), built only from S1's primitives and S1's own boolean:

- `comb K`: K disjoint cylinders along x and K along y, a smaller through a larger at a generic offset, K^2 crossings, 8K faces, up to K = 16 (K = 32 ran once locally, 7 s per boolean, not on the measuring machine).
- `spheres K`: K disjoint spheres in a row cut by one box, 2K + 6 faces, up to 256.
- `plate N`: a plate with N holes made by sequential cuts, and one more hole cut. The timed boolean is the last hole only, so the edit is the same size at every N. Up to N = 64.
- `nurbs-blocks K`: K disjoint NURBS blocks (6 by 6 control points) cut by one plane. Up to 16.
- `nurbs-rows R`: one NURBS block with an R by R control grid and a horizontal cut, up to R = 16. `R = 32` did not complete (the boolean returned a report; not investigated).

The ladders were checked by volume: `comb 2/4/8` against K^2 times one crossing, `spheres 4/16` against K times one sphere, `plate 0/1/4/16/64` against the closed form, and `nurbs-blocks 2/4` against the sum of the single blocks. All match to 2e-10 relative or better, with no bad edge (`results/ladder-check.txt`). Other sizes were not checked.

**Classifying the work.** Parallel by nature means many tasks that read only inputs and earlier phases' results, and whose results can be merged in a fixed order with no shared write. The classification of each slice, and why:

| slice | grain of a task | class | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| `setup.trims` | a face | parallel | reads the input only |
| `broad.bounds`, `broad.test` | a face; a row of the box-box test | parallel | read-only, per-row results concatenated in row order |
| `pairs.section` | a pair of surfaces | parallel | pure function of two surfaces; memoisation becomes a sort-and-dedupe of the keys before the fork |
| `pairs.hits` | an (edge, other face) pair | parallel | pure function of an edge and a surface; `locate` reads the trims only |
| `split.edge` | an edge | parallel | reads hits and the input, and appends new edges and vertices to per-edge buffers, merged in edge order by prefix sums |
| `split.clip` | a candidate face pair | parallel, **with one assumption** | reads curves, hits and the vertex table; appends pieces per pair. Parallel only if table changes during clipping are deferred to one sequential merge. Counted without the table's own time, which is `split.clip_table`. See the sensitivity column below |
| `face.one` | a face | parallel | reads the split result only |
| `classify.region` | a region | parallel | reads the other body only |
| `setup.edge_faces`, `split.group`, `split.pair_hits`, `face.index` | - | sequential as written | one loop building a map; small |
| `split.merge` | - | sequential | sort and sweep over all vertices, then union-find: a global decision. It could be a parallel sort and a parallel connected-components pass, but it is counted as sequential |
| `split.clip_table` | - | sequential | pinch vertices and seeds added to the global vertex table, with a scan of the whole table each |
| `stitch` | - | sequential | assembles one body with ids in a fixed order |
| `book` of each phase | - | sequential | the phase's own bookkeeping, by subtraction |

The classification is only as good as the design it assumes: a `split.clip` that writes the vertex table directly is sequential, and the second Amdahl column in the limits table shows that case.

**Limits.** From the one-thread timers (a limit, not a measured speed-up; the actual pipeline is P2's business):

- `A(p) = 1 / (s + (1 - s)/p)`, with `s` the share of sequential slices, the classic Amdahl limit, for `p` = 6 and in the limit (`1/s`).
- `b(p)`, a bound that also allows for task size: sequential slices, plus, for each fork-join region in pipeline order, the greedy list-schedule makespan of that region's tasks on `p` workers (the regions are `setup.trims`, `broad.bounds`, `broad.test`, `pairs` (sections and hits together), `split.edge`, `split.clip`, `face.one`, `classify.region`). `b(p)` is the speed-up this allows, computed per boolean and summed over the workload, so no two booleans run at the same time.
- `f(p)`, the same with finer tasks where the prototype allows it: a NURBS section's grid split into its 65 columns and its traced curves as separate tasks (the rest of the section is one task), and a face split's half-edge polylines as tasks of their own (the rest of the face split one task). The finer list uses equal shares of `he_poly` per polyline, an approximation.

No cost is charged for starting, joining or stealing tasks; `b` and `f` are upper bounds, and for a boolean that takes a millisecond the forking cost is not small. Amdahl's law and greedy list scheduling are textbook (Amdahl, AFIPS 1967; Graham, SIAM J. Appl. Math. 17(2), 1969). No thread scaling was measured, so the governor does not enter the limits, and the output check is that digests are identical over the repeats, at one thread only.

**Counts.** Sample counts per workload are from a local run, one repeat under the memory cap (`results/sample-counts.txt`); they are counts, not times. Time per sample divides the timers of run `regions2` by these counts. The two counters are in the binary of `regions2` only; shares and limits are the same as in the runs without them.

Inside `face.one`, 83 to 95% is `he_poly`: sampling each half-edge's curve and projecting every sample into the face's parameter
space. `setup.trims` is the same kind of work on the input faces. Together they are **61 to 67% of every analytic family's boolean** (and 56
to 68% of the largest ladder rungs below). On NURBS they are 4 to 8%, and the marching section and classify dominate.

That sampling is a fixed density in the prototype: 720 points per full turn of a section curve or ellipse (`Curve::samples`), regardless of size. An edge is sampled for every face that uses it in the trims and again in the split, and a split face samples its boundary a second time for the tiling check and each section piece twice, forward and reversed (`boolean.rs`, `split_one_face`). In near-crossed that is 17,600 points per boolean at 70 to 120 ns each, and the derivative that `on_surface` computes for each point is thrown away by `he_poly`. Ranked first below for that reason: the cost is a choice, not a law.

Reading the size ladders:

- **Comb:** cost scales with crossings; within one regime (`regions2`), 3.3 ms per crossing at K = 1 falls to 2.4 ms at K = 4 and 2.2 ms at K = 16; setup falls from 13% to 1%, as the fixed part of the job stops mattering. The shares settle by K = 8. The bound improves: `b(6)` is 5.7 at K = 16.
- **Spheres:** every share is flat with size, because every sphere repeats the same work. `s` stays under 1% from K = 4. The bound stops at about 3.3 to 3.5 at 6 because the largest task stays comparable with the rest: by the construction it is the one tool face cut by every sphere (26.5 ms of 173 ms at K = 256).
- **Plate:** the timed cut adds one hole and the time still grows linearly with the number of holes already there, at about 0.85 ms per existing hole (1.5 ms with none and 56 ms with 64): 38 times the work for the same-sized edit. From the code, the work that grows is every face being re-trimmed and re-sampled in the split, and the hits against the edges of the two big faces that hold all the holes. The share of setup rises to 25%. The per-boolean work in S1 is proportional to the model, not to the edit.
- **NURBS blocks:** classify goes from 36% at one block to 71% at sixteen, because each ray tests every NURBS face of the other body (an all-against-all with a costly ray root). The marching section is one task per block, so the share of `pairs` falls as there are more blocks and classify grows.
- **NURBS rows:** the pair phase's section share goes from 26% to 58% as the control net grows from 4 to 16 rows: the section is one task of 65 by 65 grid evaluations and curve tracing, and the bound is 1.3 at any number of cores, 2.4 at 6 and 2.6 unlimited even with the grid and each curve as a task.

At 6 workers and at unlimited workers, on every analytic family and at every size, **face split sets the ceiling**: 47 to 76% of the bound at 6 workers and 58 to 87% at unlimited. The reason is one or a few very large tasks, not sequential code. One face carries many of the section curves of the boolean: on `spheres K=256` the largest face-split task is 26.5 ms of the whole boolean's 173 ms, which by the construction is the tool face that every sphere cuts (the timers do not name faces).

On NURBS, **pair intersection sets the ceiling**: the single marching section is 55 to 74% of the bound at 6 workers (35 ms of a 61 ms boolean at R = 16). That stays true with the grid and each traced curve as separate tasks: at R = 16, `f(6)` is 2.4 and pairs is 57% of the bound. The marching chains and the longest single curve are what remain. Once there are many NURBS faces, **classify takes over** (67% of the bound at 6 workers at K = 16).

Sequential code is not the ceiling at any of these sizes at 6 cores (0.1% to 4% of the bound). It becomes visible only at very many cores on the largest model: at `comb K=16` the sequential code is 34% of the unlimited-worker bound (79% with the finer tasks, whose `f(∞)` is 139), and half of it is `stitch` and a third `pairs.book` (the deduplication maps).

With the finer tasks, on near-crossed at unlimited workers no phase dominates: pairs 31%, trims 28%, edge split 13%, face split 13%, sequential code 7%. The next granularity limits after the face split are the setup trims of a face (one per face) and the edge-against-surface tasks.

## Answer

### Where to spend optimisation effort, in order

1. **Sampling edges into parameter-space polylines, and doing it for the whole model on every boolean.** `he_poly` and `setup.trims` together are 61 to 67% of the boolean on every analytic family and 56 to 68% on the largest ladder rungs. The density is fixed at 720 points per turn of a section curve, an edge is sampled again for every face that uses it in the trims and in the split, a split face samples its boundary a second time for the tiling check and each section piece twice (forward, then reversed), and the derivative computed with each point is discarded. Because this is a loop per point, four times fewer points would take about half the time off the whole boolean by arithmetic (the 35% that is not sampling stays, the 65% that is falls to a quarter); that arithmetic assumes the cost per point stays the same, and it is not measured here, and fewer points could break tiling checks that depend on them. Two moves stack: sample less or reuse (cached trims, a stored parameter curve where one exists, evaluate in batches), and do not touch faces the boolean did not touch. The plate ladder prices the second: one more hole costs 38 times as much on a plate with 64 holes as on an empty plate, for the same edit.
2. **Pair intersection.** On analytic families this is the edge-against-surface hits (9 to 26% of the boolean), and the section curves themselves are cheap (0 to 4%). On NURBS the marching section is the largest single cost of the boolean (26% rising to 58% as the control net grows from 4 to 16 rows) and hits add 3 to 28%. Cull hits against edge bounds first; on NURBS, grid evaluation and curve tracing are the work to make faster, jointly with P5.
3. **Classify, on NURBS.** 31% of the NURBS corpus and 14 to 71% on the ladders, 5 to 11% on the analytic families. Each ray tests every face of the other body, so the share grows with the number of NURBS faces (36% with one block, 71% with sixteen). Bounds on the ray tests, or labelling one region and propagating across edges that are not section edges, removes most of it.
4. **Edge split, as a design choice more than a cost.** Under 1 to 17% of the boolean, but if its vertex table stays shared the Amdahl limit falls to 3.5 to 5.4 at 6 cores and 7 to 48 for many cores.
5. **Do not spend effort on** broad, stitch or the bookkeeping maps yet: broad is at most about 2%, stitch 0.6%, and all of the sequential code together is 0.07 to 1.5% of the boolean at these sizes. Broad is all-pairs here and will grow quadratically, so it has to change before models get large (P8); it is not where the time goes today.

### What is parallel, and the limit

Only the stitch, the vertex merge and the bookkeeping that merges per-task results must run in sequence as written: 0.07% to 1.5% of the boolean. Amdahl's limit from that alone is 5.6 to 6.0 at 6 cores and 65 to 1399 in the limit, which **is not the number to plan around**. The limit from task sizes, with the tasks as they are, is 1.3 to 4.3 at 6 cores on the corpus families and on NURBS (2.1 to 5.3 when the face split and the NURBS grid are split finer), and reaches 3.3 to 5.7 on the largest ladder rungs other than `nurbs-rows`, whose single section keeps it at 1.3. For single small booleans (a millisecond, a dozen faces) a finer grain inside the face split is needed to approach 5 at 6 cores.

**The phase that sets the ceiling is the face split on analytic models (one face holding many section loops), and the single-task marching section on NURBS** until many NURBS faces are in play, when classify takes over. At more cores than a model can use (`b(∞)` is 59 for `comb K=16`), the sequential stitch and map-building start to dominate on that model (79% of the span with the finer tasks); on `NURBS blocks K=16` the classify span still does.

## What this does not show

- A measured speed-up. Every limit here is a bound from one-thread task times, with no cost for forking, joining or memory contention. P2 measures the real pipeline.
- A kernel. S1's prototype limits apply: no fillets, cones, tori or coincident surfaces, non-rational bicubic NURBS only, a plane the only tool on a NURBS face.
- Anything about a prototype whose sampling or classification is written differently. The ranking above is the ranking for this code; the largest item in it is a choice (the sampling density and repeated sampling), so it says where cost can be removed rather than where cost is unavoidable.
- A large model. The largest measured were 518 faces (spheres K=256), 128 faces with 256 crossings (comb K=16) and a plate with 64 holes; the corpus's own largest case is much smaller. The ladders are repetitions of one interaction and are not real parts.
- Sub-millisecond behaviour: near-sphere is 33 ms for 54 booleans, below the 100 ms the protocol asks for, and the smallest ladder rungs are under 4 ms.
- Why the machine ran in two speed regimes about 9% apart. Shares are unaffected; absolute times are good to about 9%.
- Accurate per-polyline task sizes in the finer bound (`he_poly` is divided equally among a face's polylines).
- `comb K=32` on the measuring machine (it was timed once locally only; shares match K = 16) and `nurbs-rows R=32` (the boolean returned a report and was not diagnosed).
- Correctness of ladder results beyond the volume, bad-edge and repeated-digest checks listed above.

## What it decides

Nothing is decided here; the finding feeds the design and the parallel investigations:

- P2 and the boolean design: the six-phase pipeline as a map with a deterministic merge has very little sequential code left, on condition that the vertex table is not shared during clipping.
- P4 and P5: batch evaluation of curve points on surfaces and of NURBS grids is the first place SIMD can pay.
- P8 and the document layer: most of the boolean's cost scales with the whole model, so caching and localising per face is where work can be avoided.
- S3: cached trim polygons on faces would take a double-digit share off every boolean, which the layout should allow.
