# Finding: topology layout, and the data layout that lets tasks run without locks

Investigations `proving-ground.s3` and `proving-ground.p3`, measured jointly. Code and raw output are in
`spikes/s3-p3/`. Throwaway: nothing here is copied into a crate.

## Question

Which storage layout should the B-rep use, given that every entity keeps a stable id, every operation reports what it
created, changed and deleted, tasks should run without locks, and output must be identical at any thread count (L23)?
Does batching surfaces by kind and degree (L29) pay for its indirection?

## What was measured

**Model.** A synthetic boundary representation with no geometry algorithms: closed solids of genus 1, mostly quads, built by
`spikes/s3-p3/src/genmodel.rs`. At 999,599 faces it has 1.040 loops, 4.159 oriented edges, 2.080 edges
and 1.120 vertices per face. Surfaces are 60% planes, 25% cylinders and 15% NURBS of degrees 2x2, 3x3, 1x3 and 5x5.

**Layouts.** Seven tables with stable ids (kind, slot, generation), stored as array-of-structs (`aos`) or struct-of-arrays
(`soa`), each over one `Vec` per column (`vec`) or fixed-size copy-on-write chunks behind `Arc` (`cowN`, N records per
chunk, swept from 64 to 16,384). A snapshot is a clone that shares chunks; copying on first write is the path copying of
Driscoll, Sarnak, Sleator and Tarjan (1989).

**Operations.** Edits are Euler operators (Baumgart 1975; Mantyla 1988): split an edge, add a face, delete a face, inside a
transaction with an undo log and a history of what was created, changed and deleted. NURBS evaluation is A2.1, A2.2 and
A4.3 of Piegl and Tiller (1997). Sample points are the R2 sequence (Roberts 2018).

**Machine.** Intel Core i7-8700K, 6 physical cores (CPUs 0 to 5 only, pinned with `taskset`), governor `powersave`,
transparent huge pages `always`. `rustc` 1.99.0, `x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu`, fat LTO, one codegen unit, `RUSTFLAGS` empty
unless a table says otherwise. Single-core turbo is higher than all-core turbo, so speed-ups read a little low; they are
not corrected.

**Protocol.** Seven repetitions per configuration, through `spikes/tools/remote.sh`. Tables give medians.
`spikes/s3-p3/summary.txt` gives minimum, median and spread for all 1,121 configurations, one results
file at a time. Commands are in `spikes/s3-p3/measure.sh`. Parallel work uses `std::thread::scope`, a
shared counter handing out tasks of 1,024 faces, and one output buffer per task. The spike uses `std` and the path crates
`occam-math` and `occam-units`, and `#![forbid(unsafe_code)]` everywhere except the counting allocator of the memory harness.

**Where this departs from the protocol.**

- *Spread.* 28 of the 1,106 configurations outside `growth` have a median more than 5% above their minimum, the worst 21%.
  Most are the presized read at 6 threads and edits after a snapshot. I did not chase each one. All 15 `growth`
  configurations exceed it by design, because only some repetitions hit a reallocation; that table gives both values.
- *Duration.* Edit repetitions are a sum of separately timed rounds, sized from a cold first round, so they total 3.7 to
  234 ms (median 78 ms), and 71% fall under the 100 ms the protocol asks for. Their spread is small all the same: median
  1.0% among the configurations with a repetition under 50 ms, 10.7% at worst. The shortest one-thread repetitions of
  `parphase` (74 ms at 100,000 faces, 97 ms at a million) and `batchkind` (77 ms) also fall short. Each timed k=1 edit includes its clock reads.
- *Sizes.* Parallel measures cover 10^5 and 10^6 faces only.
- *Provenance of results.* Most files carry a `# source=` commit. Those without one (`info`, `snapshot`, `traverse-*`, `thp-*`,
  `jcc-star`, `growth`) were measured from the code first committed in `85e310f`, or just before it; the later commits only add
  experiments and a reservation that those paths do not call.

**Faults fixed before these numbers were taken.** Five, all in the harness, and every affected section was measured after its
fix. The `Vec` layouts were charged a whole-table reallocation on their first edit, which `growth` now measures on its own.
The lock baseline for parallel edits alone lost its spare capacity on cloning. Mixed-order surface evaluation grew its buffers
from empty while batch reserved them. A digest that was an exclusive-or over rounds would have cancelled to zero for an even
count, and is now folded. The grouped surface store was accounted with its growth slack still in it.

## Answer

1. **Use copy-on-write chunks, not plain arenas.** At a million faces a snapshot takes 47 microseconds against 31 milliseconds,
   a snapshot plus one edit takes 92 microseconds against 33 milliseconds, and a second version costs 0.27% more memory against
   100%. The price is 15 to 46% on coherent traversal and 11 to 33% on an edit in place, and chunks avoid a `Vec`'s 1.3 to 25
   millisecond reallocation spike.
2. **Chunks of 1,024 to 4,096 records.** Below 1,024 the snapshot itself gets expensive (725 microseconds at 256, 3.9
   milliseconds at 64). Above 4,096, copying does (149 microseconds per edit at 16,384 for scattered edits). 4,096 is best for
   a snapshot and one edit; 1,024 for a transaction whose edits scatter (14 against 41 microseconds per edit, and 3.4% against
   12.5% more memory).
3. **Struct-of-arrays inside the chunks is the better default, narrowly.** It scales further on reads (4.83x against 3.23x at 6
   threads with no output, 2.42x against 1.86x with a reused output) and walks faster (2.48 against 3.55 ns). It loses on
   snapshots (314 against 47 microseconds), on edits in place (1.98 against 1.37 microseconds scattered), and on bounds in
   scrambled order (379 against 297 ns), probably because a vertex's three coordinates are then three cache misses.
4. **Immutable snapshots plus per-task buffers merged in a stable order are right.** Output was bit-identical at every thread
   count, on every layout. A copy-on-write snapshot costs nothing a reader can see (31.3 against 31.1 ns per face). At 6 threads the lock
   baselines are 3.6 to 9 times slower than the lock-free read of the same layout, and lock-based edits gave a different history on
   every run above one thread. At one
   thread the lock-based edit was 18% faster: the case against locks is determinism and contention, not their uncontended cost.
5. **The merge is the thing to design.** At a million faces on 6 cores, lock-free reads reach 1.5 to 2.0x with buffers and a
   sequential merge, 1.6 to 2.4x without the merge, and 2.7 to 4.8x with no output at all. Where output size is known in advance, write in place into a
   presized output using `chunks_mut`, which needs no lock, no atomic and no `unsafe`. Heavier per-face work scales much
   better: the tasks of surface evaluation reach 5.4 to 5.6x.
6. **L29 holds for memory and for NURBS on AVX2, and not more widely.** Grouping saves 15% of the surface memory. With AVX2,
   lanes make NURBS 23 to 27% faster, and choosing the best variant per kind would give about 19% off the mixed cost (computed
   from the per-kind table, not measured as a whole). On baseline x86-64 nothing is established to be faster. Lanes do not help
   planes or cylinders on either target. Batching adds 7 to 9 ns per face to the sequential merge, where results arrive in group
   order rather than face order.

## What this does not show

- Nothing here runs real geometry. Edit work is about a microsecond of bookkeeping, and read work is about 20 ns per face. A real
  plan or intersection may be orders of magnitude heavier, which would make the merge and the apply negligible. The parallel
  read figures describe a memory-bound microbenchmark.
- Memory bandwidth is the likeliest limit on the read, but it was inferred from the phases and never measured.
- One machine, x86-64, six cores. No browser, no wasm, no second memory configuration. O9 covers wider hardware.
- One thread runner, `std::thread::scope`, spawning threads for every pass. A pool would save that, most at 100,000 faces.
  `proving-ground` has a separate investigation for this.
- Locks were tried per face or per record, not as a well-tuned design with batching.
- The `presized` variant at a million faces gets slower from 2 to 6 threads. The allocator returns untouched pages, so page faults
  land inside the tasks. That is consistent with what the phases show, but I did not measure page faults.
- A small unexplained effect: `snapshot` plans cost 8 ns per face more than `borrow` at one thread on copy-on-write, and nothing at
  two threads or more.
- Fields were not split into hot and cold columns, which might recover some of what struct-of-arrays loses on edits.

## What it decides

For `proving-ground.design`, as proposals to confirm there:

- `occam-topo`: copy-on-write chunks of 1,024 to 4,096 records with stable ids (kind, slot, generation); readers work from an
  immutable snapshot; writes happen only in a sequential apply.
- Tasks produce per-task results merged in a stable order, or write in place where the size is known. Ids are assigned at the
  merge, never under a lock. This is what makes L23 hold.
- `occam-geom`: store surfaces grouped by kind and degree, evaluate planes and cylinders in scalar, and use lanes for NURBS only.

## Open questions for the user

1. **Struct-of-arrays or array-of-structs inside chunks?** I recommend struct-of-arrays for its read scaling, knowing it makes
   edits about 45% dearer. The numbers do not force it, and hot and cold columns were not tried.
2. **Should L29 be narrowed?** I recommend recording it as "batch NURBS by degree, for lanes, on targets with wide vectors, and
   group surfaces by kind to save memory", because the wider claim did not hold here.
3. **Should the kernel get x86-64-v3 code paths?** The NURBS gain exists only there. Runtime dispatch means either hand-written
   detection or a new dependency, and new dependencies are the user's call; a v3-only build would exclude older CPUs. I recommend
   leaving this until a real kernel profile shows NURBS evaluation is worth it.
