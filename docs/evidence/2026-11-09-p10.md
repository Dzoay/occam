# proving-ground.p10: what runs in the browser?

In short: ship a worker pool with separate memories, built on stable Rust with simd128. It needs no special hosting. Wasm
threads were 2 to 14% faster warm on the quiet machine and no faster on a cold request, and they cost a nightly toolchain,
COOP/COEP on every page, and hand-written thread start-up. O10 does not need deciding now.

## Question

Which of these should occam's first release run in the browser, and at what sizes does it matter (L30, L33)? One thread with
simd128; a worker pool with separate memories, copying flat typed arrays; or wasm threads over `SharedArrayBuffer`, which
need COOP/COEP hosting and a nightly toolchain (O10 in `docs/PLAN.md`). The full statement is in `spikes/p10/QUESTION.md`.

## What was measured

**Machines.** The measuring machine is an Intel Core i7-8700K with six physical cores (CPUs 0 to 5 used, hyperthreads never),
governor `powersave`. Single-core turbo is higher than all-core turbo, so speed-ups read a little low. It ran Firefox 159.0.1
headless and node v26.13.0. A laptop (Intel Core i7-1165G7, four physical cores, `powersave`, Chromium 156.0.8078.79 and
Firefox 159.0.1) is not quiet, and is used only as a cross-check on differences between browsers.

**Builds.** All are `--release` with `lto = "fat"`, `codegen-units = 1` and no `target-cpu`. Exact commands are in
`spikes/p10/scripts/build.sh`.

| Build | Toolchain | Target | Flags |
|---|---|---|---|
| native | rustc 1.99.0 | `x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu` | none |
| scalar | rustc 1.99.0 | `wasm32-unknown-unknown` | none |
| simd128 | rustc 1.99.0 | `wasm32-unknown-unknown` | `-C target-feature=+simd128` |
| threads | nightly-2026-11-01 (rustc 1.101.0-nightly f9ceb4a4d) | `wasm32-unknown-unknown` | `-Zbuild-std=std,panic_abort`, `-C target-feature=+atomics,+bulk-memory,+mutable-globals,+simd128`, eight linker arguments |

**Workloads,** written once over `occam_math::Real` and `Lanes<W>`:

- Even: a bicubic B-spline surface, 256 by 256 control points, evaluated over a 1500 by 1500 grid (2.25 million points, 54 MB
  of output). FindSpan, BasisFuns and SurfacePoint are Algorithms A2.1, A2.2 and A3.5 of Piegl and Tiller, *The NURBS Book*
  (2nd ed., 1997); the recurrence is de Boor's (*A Practical Guide to Splines*, 1978).
- Uneven: 240,000 cubic Bézier curves flattened by adaptive de Casteljau bisection (*The NURBS Book* A1.5), to the flatness
  bound of Filip, Magedson and Markot (CAGD 3, 1986). 79.2% are cheap, 20% medium and 0.8% very hard, with the hard ones
  clustered. It is uneven as intended: on native at six threads, dynamic scheduling took 54.5 ms and a static split 112.8 ms.

**Candidates.**

1. One thread: one instance on one worker. The page's main thread never runs wasm.
2. Pool: N workers, each with its own instance and memory. The page hands out 64 chunks dynamically. Each worker gets one
   copy of the input; each chunk's output is copied out of linear memory, transferred to the page and merged.
3. Threads: one shared memory, a leader worker that also computes, and N - 1 helper workers that block in wasm when idle. The
   64 chunks are pulled from an atomic counter. The output is copied once to an unshared array.

**Protocol.** Minimum and median of seven repeats after warm-up, unless a row says otherwise. Native and node are pinned to
CPUs `0` to `n-1`. A browser needs a core for its own threads, so Firefox's one-worker sweep uses CPUs 0-1, and its
single-thread matrix uses 0-5: pinned to CPU 0 alone, the background compiler shared the core and spread reached 52%
(`firefox-single-pin0.jsonl`, kept but not used). Firefox sweeps use ten warm-up runs (`ffsweep*.jsonl`); an earlier pass
with three is kept as `firefox-sweep*.jsonl` and shows why, with spreads up to 43%. Scripts are in `spikes/p10/scripts/`, raw
output in `spikes/p10/results/`, and the numbers below come from `scripts/summarise.py` or directly from those files.
`scripts/measure-browsers.sh` records the commands.

**simd128 was not slower than scalar, so L30 did not recur here.** On the quiet machine it was level to 14% faster on the
surface and 21 to 24% faster on the uneven workload. The uneven workload does not use `Lanes`, so that gain comes from the
compiler's use of simd128; the mechanism was not investigated. The one apparent counter-example was the laptop's first
Firefox pass, where simd128 took about twice as long as scalar (808 against 428 ms) under a load average near 9. Alternating
reruns on the same laptop had them level, the cleanest pair being 384 against 375 ms (`laptop-firefox-simd-recheck.jsonl`),
so that was load.

Lanes wider than the hardware's 128 bits never helped, in wasm or on generic x86-64 native. Against native, the best wasm
build costs 1.06 times on the surface and 1.08 on the uneven workload in node, and 1.34 and 1.04 in Firefox.

**Against the recommendation:** on Chromium on the laptop at four workers, threads led by more, 16 to 18% on the surface and

node's threads surface row at four (5.9%). In Firefox the repeats fall steadily (85, 70, 77, 60, 65, 56, 58 ms), so warm-up
was still going on. Giving the browser one spare core cut the four-worker spread to 5.2% (`ffsweep4-spare.jsonl`). That
points to the page's main thread and the background compiler competing with the workers; it was not proven.

- **Small requests.** Everything takes tens of milliseconds, and of the three candidates threads take longest to start. The
  spread here is large (the minimum and median differ by up to four times, in both start-up and compute), so read these rows
  for order of magnitude only.
- **Large requests.** The pool is level with threads or ahead of them in every environment, by 1 to 15% on the median. On the
  surface, 24 ms of Firefox's 186 is the copy out of shared memory. Without it threads would lead the pool by 5% there.
- **One long call is a trap in Firefox.** A single export call covering the whole job took 1.4 times as long as the same
  single worker split into 64 calls on the surface, and 2.3 times on the uneven workload. Node shows the reverse, 7 to 9% in
  favour of the single call, from the cost of moving the chunks. The likely cause is that Firefox's tiered compiler can only
  start using its optimised code at a new call, but that was inferred from this pattern and not profiled.
- **So the honest cold speed-up from six workers in Firefox is 4.6 times on the surface and 4.1 on the uneven workload**
  (median, against one worker with 64 calls), not the 6.5 and 9.6 that comparing against one long call would suggest.

### Idle cost of threads

Six workers, helpers with nothing to do. `Spin limit` is how many times a helper checks for work before it blocks. CPU is for
the whole browser or node process during one 3 s window, over seven windows; dispatch is the time to wake every helper and
collect them for an empty job. Files are `firefox-idle7.jsonl` and `node-idle7.jsonl`.

| Env | Spin limit | Idle CPU ms, min / median / max | Dispatch us per round, min / median |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firefox | 0 | 50 / 100 / 2470 | 10.45 / 11.19 |
| Firefox | 2,000 | 20 / 40 / 60 | 6.94 / 7.53 |
| Firefox | 200,000 | 20 / 50 / 60 | 3.75 / 4.09 |
| Firefox | 2,000,000,000 | 4850 / 6980 / 11290 | 3.68 / 4.01 |
| Node | 0 | 0.2 / 0.3 / 0.8 | 10.10 / 78.49 |
| Node | 2,000 | 0.2 / 0.2 / 0.3 | 9.83 / 61.21 |
| Node | 200,000 | 0.2 / 0.2 / 0.4 | 3.74 / 4.15 |
| Node | 2,000,000,000 | 0.1 / 0.1 / 0.3 | 3.90 / 4.15 |
| Node, no settling time | 2,000,000,000 | 4344 / 4348 / 10784 | 3.85 / 4.04 |

Blocked helpers cost nothing measurable. Node reads 0.1 to 0.8 ms of CPU in a 3 s window. Firefox's medians of 40 to 100 ms
look like the browser's own background activity: the three outliers (600 to 2470 ms) all came within about 30 s of the page
loading and did not recur.

Spinning costs whole cores. In Firefox a budget of 2 billion spins burned 4.9 to 11.3 s of CPU inside a 3 s window. Node uses
up the same budget in about 0.9 s per helper, so after 1 s of settling its row shows helpers that have already blocked.
Measured from the moment the pool exists, its five helpers burned 4.3 s (`node-idlespin.jsonl`; the first of seven windows
read 10.8 s, probably because the code was not yet optimised). Node's reading does count worker threads: two workers spinning
for 1.5 s read 3090 ms (`scripts/check-cpu-meter.mjs`).

Waking blocked helpers costs about 7 us more per dispatch than catching them spinning in Firefox, and about 60 to 75 us more
in node (medians; node's minimum is about 10 us, and that spread was not explained), against requests of tens of milliseconds
or more. So blocking is the right default: spinning through a long idle period burns whole cores for a saving nobody would
notice.

### Hosting, and what the headers break

Measured with a page and a separate third-party origin on localhost (`web/headers.html`). Firefox 159 and Chromium 156 agreed
on every row, and Firefox agreed with itself on both machines.

| Test | COOP plus `COEP: require-corp` | COOP plus `COEP: credentialless` | No headers |
|---|---|---|---|
| `crossOriginIsolated` | true | true | false |
| `SharedArrayBuffer` defined | yes | yes | no |
| Wasm shared memory sent to a worker | works | works | `DataCloneError` |
| Third-party image, no CORP header | blocked | loads | loads |
| Third-party script, no headers | blocked | runs | runs |
| Third-party iframe, no CORP or COEP | did not run | did not run | runs |
| Third-party iframe, with CORP and COEP | runs | runs | runs |

So threads need `Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy: same-origin` and a `Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy` on the page, and that blocks
every third-party iframe which has not opted in. `credentialless` rescues images and scripts but not iframes. By MDN's
compatibility data (fetched 2026-11-09), Safari does not support `credentialless` at all, which would leave `require-corp`
there; Safari was not tested.

**The pool needs none of this.** It ran on a page served with no COOP or COEP headers, with the same digests and the same
speed: 40 to 41 ms on the surface and 59 ms on the uneven workload at six workers (`firefox-pool-plain.jsonl`, 1 ms timer
resolution).

**The main thread.** `Atomics.wait` from the page's main thread throws in both browsers. Blocking in wasm differs: Chromium
traps at once (`RuntimeError: Atomics.wait cannot be called in this context`), and Firefox 159 really blocks the page (it
returned after 1000 ms with a helper delayed by 1000 ms). On the laptop, one Firefox run at no delay hung until the 30 s
timeout; that happened once and was not explained. Outside that test, the harness never calls the shared module from the page: a
leader worker takes part in the work and blocks, and the page only exchanges messages with it. Rust's own wasm allocator
spins rather than blocks for this reason (its comment in `library/std/src/sys/alloc/wasm.rs` says the main thread "*cannot
ever block*, no exceptions").

### Build pain and stability

- Stable Rust cannot build it. With the same flags, rustc warns that `atomics` is an unstable target feature and the link
  fails, because the pre-built `std` was not compiled with `atomics` or `bulk-memory` (`results/stable-atomics-attempt.txt`).
- Nightly needs one pinned toolchain with `rust-src`, `-Zbuild-std=std,panic_abort`, four target features, and eight linker
  arguments passed by hand (`--shared-memory`, `--import-memory`, `--max-memory`, and exports of `__stack_pointer`,
  `__tls_size`, `__tls_align`, `__tls_base` and `__wasm_init_tls`), because rustc adds none of them for this target. A build
  takes 17 s on six cores.
- `std::thread::spawn` is not available here. In this `std` the only thread function the wasm-with-atomics module provides is
  `sleep`. So the helpers are browser workers, and hand-written JS must allocate each helper's stack and thread-local block,
  set `__stack_pointer` and call `__wasm_init_tls`. Those are linker conventions, not a Rust contract.
- The web half looks settled, the Rust half does not. `SharedArrayBuffer` has shipped since Chrome 68, Firefox 79 and Safari
  15.2 (MDN data), and the Threads proposal is at Phase 4 of the WebAssembly process, not yet Phase 5
  (`WebAssembly/proposals`, fetched 2026-11-09). The Cargo book for nightly still calls `-Z build-std` "in the very early
  stages of development".
- The first threads build linked without complaint and produced a module with its own unshared memory, because those linker
  arguments were missing. Nothing warns you, and only inspecting the module's imports showed it.

## Answer

**Ship the worker pool with separate memories, on stable Rust with simd128.** Threads in the browser do not pay for
themselves at the sizes measured.

- Warm, at six workers on the quiet machine, threads lead the pool by 2 to 14% in Firefox and 2 to 4% in node once the output
  is copied out. They led by 11 to 18% on the laptop's Chromium, the one reading that favours them more, on a machine that is
  not quiet.
- Cold, which is what an interactive request looks like the first time, the pool is level or ahead at large sizes in all
  three environments, and threads take longest to start at small sizes.
- For that, threads would cost COOP/COEP on every page (and the third-party content it blocks), a nightly toolchain with
  `-Zbuild-std`, hand-written stack and thread-local start-up that leans on linker conventions, and a rule that the page must
  never call the module.

How to build it, whichever candidate is chosen later:

1. Do no wasm work on the page's main thread.
2. Keep the pool alive between requests. Start-up is small but is most of the cost of a small request.
3. Split every job into many short calls. Here 64 chunks both balanced the uneven work (dynamic scheduling was twice as fast
   as static on native) and avoided Firefox's long-call penalty of 1.4 to 2.3 times.
4. Pass flat typed arrays and transfer them. Transfers are nearly free, and clones cost about 0.3 ms per MiB.

One thread with simd128 is the fallback and the floor: it is close to native in node and on the uneven workload in Firefox,
1.34 times native on the surface in Firefox.

**Hosting.** Any static host, with no COOP or COEP. The `.wasm` file must be served as `application/wasm` for
`compileStreaming`, which is what the test server does.

**O10 does not need deciding now.** Nothing in the first release needs nightly. Reopen it if a real kernel finds one of the
cases this spike could not test: a model large enough that a copy per worker hurts, or tasks fine-grained enough that message
passing dominates. It would also be worth reopening when `-Zbuild-std` stabilises, since that removes most of the build cost.

## What this does not show

- It has no real CAD workload. These are surface evaluation and curve flattening, with 1.57 MB of shared input. A boolean
  over a large model, where every worker needs the whole model, is where the pool's copies would cost most. Peak memory was
  not measured: the pool holds a copy of the input per worker, and threads hold one.
- Only 64 chunks were tried. How small a task can be before message passing dominates the pool is not known.
- Chromium was measured only on a laptop that is not quiet, and only at four workers. Safari and mobile browsers were not
  measured at all.
- The reason for Firefox's long-call penalty is inferred, not profiled. The reason simd128 speeds up the uneven workload was
  not investigated, and Firefox's 9% gap between the threads build and the pool's per-worker compute at one worker (170
  against 186 ms) was not explained.
- Compiles used `cache: no-store`. A deployed page with a cached module would start differently.
- Start-up per worker has one sample per worker count, though the cold-request tables give seven for six workers.
- COOP's effect on cross-origin popups, such as a sign-in window, was not tested. That `credentialless` sends no credentials
  is the documented behaviour, also not tested.

## What it decides

- Subject to `proving-ground.design` and the questions below, the first release's wasm facade targets a worker pool with
  separate memories and flat typed arrays, confirming the direction of L33.
- simd128 is the default wasm build. L30's slowdown did not recur, though the lesson stands that this must be measured from
  the first kernel.
- Hosting asks nothing special of a deployment beyond serving `.wasm` as `application/wasm`.
- The determinism promise in `docs/DETERMINISM.md` held across every candidate here, including shared-memory threads, which
  it does not yet list.

## Open questions for the user

1. **Should the first release require nothing of its host (no COOP/COEP)?** Recommended: yes. It keeps occam embeddable next
   to third-party content. The cost is giving up a 2 to 14% warm speed-up on the quiet machine.
2. **Keep O10 open, with the triggers above?** Recommended: yes, and do not take on nightly for real use now.
3. **Which browsers must the first release support?** Safari was not measured here. Recommended: measure it before any
   release that claims to support it, since its missing `credentialless` only matters if threads ever ship.
